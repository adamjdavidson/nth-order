# Nth Order

Use a disciplined Futures Wheel-style analysis to turn a change into decision-relevant consequences. Optimize for better strategic choices, not a confident prediction of one future.

Example request: “AI makes accounting, payroll, and audit preparation close to real-time and low-cost. Analyze the implications for a mid-market company and its industry.”

## Set the decision frame

1. Restate the input as one neutral change statement. Distinguish the capability itself from its adoption, cost, access, reliability, and regulation.
2. Identify the decision that the analysis should inform. If the user does not state one, frame the result around how the organization should position, invest, partner, or prepare.
3. State the company, industry, geography, and time-horizon assumptions that materially affect the result. Proceed with explicit assumptions unless a missing fact could reverse the recommendation.
4. Read `references/methods.md` when choosing between consequence mapping, scenarios, systems feedback, or transaction-cost analysis.

## Establish the evidence boundary

- Research only when a real, time-sensitive external fact would materially change the result. Prefer primary, official, or high-quality research sources and cite them inline.
- Do not browse merely to decorate a conceptual or hypothetical analysis.
- Label claims as **Evidence**, **Inference**, or **Assumption**. Keep facts from inferences visibly separate.
- Treat time horizons as estimates driven by adoption and constraints, not as definitions of the impact order.

## Map the cascade

Create a compact causal impact map with a few material, diverse branches. For each node, name the effect, causal mechanism, affected actors, relevant condition, rough timing, and confidence.

Complete each order across the material branches before extending any branch outward. Do not trace one attractive chain all the way to the end while ignoring alternatives.

### First order — direct change

Identify immediate shifts in capability, cost, speed, quality, accessibility, or risk. Describe the direct mechanism; do not jump straight from a capability to an employment or market conclusion.

### Second order — behavioral and economic response

Trace how customers, employees, competitors, suppliers, and investors change behavior once the direct change becomes usable or widespread. Examine demand elasticity, experimentation, pricing, complementarity, substitution, and new bottlenecks.

### Third order — structure and value capture

Examine changes to industry structure, profit pools, operating models, organizational boundaries, governance, and institutions. Use transaction-cost economics only when the change affects search, contracting, verification, coordination, monitoring, or integration costs; also account for trust, incentives, proprietary assets, regulation, and integration costs.

### Fourth order — conditional watchpoints

Use fourth-order effects only for plausible regime shifts, feedback loops, or institutional changes that could reshape earlier layers. State each as:

`If [conditions hold], then [regime shift or feedback]; watch for [observable signal].`

Never present a fourth-order effect as inevitable. Omit it when no decision-relevant, defensible watchpoint exists.

## Apply systems and scenario discipline

- Look for reinforcing and balancing feedback loops, delays, thresholds, bottlenecks, and countervailing responses. Show only the few that alter a strategic choice.
- Cover relevant lenses: customer behavior, unit economics and value pools, labor and capabilities, firm boundaries, competition and ecosystems, regulation, trust, and legitimacy.
- Create two or three alternative scenarios only when a critical uncertainty changes the recommended action. Name the uncertainty, describe the divergent conditions, and stress-test the options; do not assign unsupported probabilities or build scenarios by default.

## Turn the map into strategy

Separate moves by commitment and reversibility:

- **No-regrets actions:** valuable across plausible futures.
- **Targeted bets:** valuable if named assumptions prove true.
- **Options to preserve:** partnerships, capabilities, data, or rights that keep future moves open.
- **Risks and blockers:** constraints that could invalidate the thesis.
- **Leading indicators:** observable signals that distinguish the important branches.

## Produce the response

Use **Executive Brief** by default. Keep it selective enough for a five-to-ten-minute executive read.

1. **Decision frame** — central change, decision, scope, and material assumptions.
2. **Strategic thesis** — the most consequential non-obvious implication and why it matters.
3. **Causal impact map** — group concise, linked effects under First order, Second order, Third order, and conditional Fourth order watchpoints. Include timing and confidence without inventing precision.
4. **Strategic choices** — no-regrets actions, targeted bets, and options to preserve.
5. **Uncertainties and signals** — counterforces, disconfirming evidence, and leading indicators.
6. **Executive questions** — three to five questions that would materially improve the next decision.

Use **Strategy Memo** when the user asks for a deep dive. Expand the brief with an evidence inventory, stakeholder-specific consequences, explicit feedback loops and delays, a scenario stress test, and a more detailed operating-model or industry-structure analysis.

## Avoid weak analysis

- Do not equate automation with job elimination; examine task composition, demand, complements, control, and new coordination work.
- Do not assume universal, instantaneous adoption or zero marginal cost without stating the constraints required.
- Do not use order labels as a substitute for causal mechanisms.
- Do not bury the decision in an exhaustive list of possibilities. Prioritize branches that could change action, ownership, or timing.
- Do not confuse a plausible narrative with a forecast. Surface the conditions under which the narrative fails.
